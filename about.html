What is openpathogen, and how does it work?

Developed by Amulya Baral

openpathogen is an open-source, privacy-friendly, zero-install pathogen profiling platform.

It uses KMA (read-based gene detection), version 1.5.1, compiled to WebAssembly.

All of your data is analyzed locally in your own browser, and is never uploaded anywhere. This makes it fast, private, and safe to use even with sensitive clinical or unpublished sequencing data.

Because everything runs client-side, there is no account to create and no server that you have to put your trust on. The app itself, the WebAssembly engines and the databases are the only things downloaded to you — the exact, complete list of network traffic is in the privacy section below.

Databases are downloaded once, verified against pinned SHA-256 checksums, and cached in your browser (IndexedDB), so repeat runs against the same database start instantly. The first run against a large database may take a moment while it downloads.

Privacy: exactly what leaves your browser

Nothing you upload ever leaves your machine — but a website is never zero-network, and you should not have to take our word for it. This is the complete list of requests openpathogen makes, so it can be checked against the open-source code or your browser's network inspector.

Your sequencing files (FASTQ)
Never uploaded, never sent anywhere, never written to disk. Files are read into memory, processed by WebAssembly inside your browser, and gone when you close the tab. No cookies, no accounts, no local storage of your files.
Page views (Cloudflare, our host)
This site is served through Cloudflare, which handles every request — including your IP address — the way any web host must. Page views are counted with Cloudflare's built-in Web Analytics: cookieless, no cross-site tracking, no advertising, no selling of data, and when enabled Cloudflare injects its own small beacon script (visible in your network inspector as cloudflareinsights.com). There is no separate analytics provider — no third-party tracker runs on every visit, and no analytics code ships in the app's own source. The only requests that leave this site go to ENA (when you fetch a run) or Zenodo (databases), both below.
Databases, CABBAGE snapshot & engines (this site / Zenodo)
The KMA/fastp engines ship with the page; the ResFinder, CARD and VFDB indexes and the CABBAGE phenotype snapshot are downloaded once from this site or Zenodo and cached locally. Zenodo sees the download request; every file is checked against a pinned SHA-256 checksum before it is used or cached, so a corrupted or tampered download is rejected rather than silently changing your results.
Fetching a public run (only when you ask)
If you fetch a run by accession or load the example isolate, the accession and your IP address are sent to EMBL-EBI (ENA) — that is inherent in downloading from them, and it means ENA knows which public run you fetched. The reads are then cached in your browser like the databases.

That is everything. To remove all locally cached data (indexes, snapshots, example and fetched runs), use Clear cached data under the database selector on the main page.

Getting started

  1. Choose your input type: paired-end, single-end, or Nanopore. In Advanced mode you also choose the reference database; Simple mode runs all of them.
  2. Select your read file(s) (.fastq or gzipped .fastq.gz), or load the example isolate — S. aureus USA300_TCH1516 (SRR10341524), a 48 MB MiSeq run of the community-associated MRSA reference strain, downloaded straight from ENA and cached in your browser. You should see mecA, blaZ plus aminoglycoside and macrolide genes on ResFinder, and PVL/hla on VFDB. You can also fetch any public run by its accession (SRR…, ERR…, DRR…) with Or fetch a public run; the organism and read type are detected automatically, and files are limited to 1 GB each.
  3. Start the analysis. Live progress is streamed into the Logs panel, which you can expand at any time to watch the KMA command and its output.

CABBAGE: from genotype to phenotype

Gene detection answers what is there; clinicians usually need what it means for treatment. Every analysis therefore ends with a Phenotype prediction (CABBAGE) card, built on CABBAGE (Comprehensive Assessment of Bacterial-Based AMR prediction from Genotypes, Dickens et al., NAR 2026) — the database linking 170,000+ sequenced bacterial isolates to their measured antimicrobial susceptibility phenotypes (~1.7 million AST records).

For the sample's species, each detected gene is matched against the ~165,000 CABBAGE isolates that have both a genome and an antibiogram, so results read like: "mecA — 97.7% of 1,791 S. aureus isolates carrying this gene were methicillin-resistant", with the species' background resistance rate shown alongside for comparison, and phenotypes taken from the updated 2025 CLSI/EUCAST breakpoint reinterpretation. A species antibiogram below the predictions shows per-antibiotic resistance rates across the whole database, refinable by country, year and isolation source. Genes with no phenotype evidence in CABBAGE are listed honestly rather than guessed. Everything is computed locally from a small cached snapshot, and the predictions ship inside the results ZIP; to browse the full database itself, use the EMBL-EBI AMR portal.

Some notes

  • Defaults of 80% identity and 80% coverage have been chosen following common reporting thresholds; this does not in any way mean they are absolute. For example, you can lower the identity threshold to recover divergent variants, or lower the coverage threshold for fragmented or low-depth samples.
  • Nanopore mode enables KMA's -bcNano for indel-aware base calling.
  • You can click any detected gene in the results to inspect its coverage, consensus sequence, alignment, and variants.
  • The output bundle includes .res, .aln, .fsa, .frag.gz, .mat.gz, .vcf.gz, and .mapstat.
  • openpathogen needs a modern browser with SharedArrayBuffer support (recent Chrome, Firefox, Edge, or Safari).
